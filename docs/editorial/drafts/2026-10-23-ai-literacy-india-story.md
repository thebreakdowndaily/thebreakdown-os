# Indian Students Are Using AI to Learn. What Should Schools Teach Them Next?

*A ten-state survey found that 35% of children who already use educational technology also use generative AI for learning. The same report records a gap between awareness and how children describe the technology. It does not establish whether they can detect incorrect answers—or whether AI improves measured learning.*

## Two numbers, one question

In the executive summary of the *Bharat Survey for EdTech (BaSE) 2025* report, two findings sit close together. Among children who had heard of generative AI, 85% said they understood how it worked. Yet 72% of that same aware group described it as an internet-search application. [1]

The figures are striking, but they are not the result of a technical examination. The survey reports what respondents said about their understanding and their conception of the tool; it does not show students taking a test to explain how a chatbot generates an answer, check a citation, or spot a false claim. The contrast is a signal to investigate—not proof that children cannot use AI well.

That distinction matters as Indian schools begin a new phase of formal teaching about computational thinking and artificial intelligence. The public question is no longer only whether young people can access a chatbot. It is whether schools can help them decide when to trust an answer, when to check it and when to put the tool aside.

## AI use has entered existing study routines

BaSE 2025, published by the education non-profit Central Square Foundation (CSF) in February 2026, covers 12,500 households and 2,500 teachers across ten states. The study focuses on children and teachers in low-income settings, including government and affordable private schools. It is substantial multi-state evidence, but it should not be presented as a census of every student or school in India. [1][2]

The report says 63% of children in surveyed households used educational technology. Among children already using EdTech, 50% had heard of generative AI, 44% knew it could be used for learning, and 35% reported using it for learning. These percentages have the same broad base—EdTech-using children—but describe different steps: awareness, awareness of a learning use, and actual reported use. [1]

For the children who use generative AI, the tool is not necessarily an occasional novelty. BaSE reports that 69% of GenAI-using children used it daily, and 96% used it multiple times a week. The most commonly reported learning use was doubt-solving and practice, at 73%. Forty-eight per cent used it to learn a language or skill or to translate content, while 32% used it for test preparation. Respondents could report more than one use, so the categories should not be added together. [1]

The route into the technology is also revealing. Sixty-eight per cent of GenAI-using children said peers introduced them to it; 46% had heard about it from school or teachers. Those channels can overlap. The figures suggest that friends and classmates are an important route of discovery, but they do not establish what students learned from those conversations or how accurately they understand the tool. [1]

The survey therefore documents adoption and self-reported experience. It does not, on its own, establish students’ ability to verify an answer, the accuracy of any particular AI product, or the effect of use on long-term achievement.

## Why confidence and understanding are not the same measure

A student may know how to open a chatbot, ask a question and receive a clear explanation without having a reliable mental model of how the system works. That is a reasonable interpretation of the contrast in BaSE—but it remains an interpretation, not a measured finding about each child.

Generative AI can produce fluent text without guaranteeing that every statement is accurate, current or supported by evidence. Some tools can search the web or display citations. Those features can help, but a link still needs to be checked: does it open, does it support the exact claim, and does it come from a source qualified to establish it?

A useful lesson would teach students to ask a few practical questions before using an answer in schoolwork:

- What exact claim am I relying on?
- Can I find an original source, textbook passage, official document or qualified explanation that supports it?
- Does the cited source actually say what the chatbot says it says?
- What is uncertain, outdated or missing from the answer?
- Can I explain the idea in my own words without looking at the generated response?

These are proposed teaching practices, not skills measured by the BaSE survey. Whether Indian students currently do them—and how often they need support—requires direct classroom research.

## The school curriculum is beginning to respond

The Central Board of Secondary Education (CBSE) lists a 2026–27 curriculum in Computational Thinking and Artificial Intelligence, along with student and teacher resource books for Classes III–VIII. Its circular of 9 April 2026 describes computational thinking as a foundation for learning about AI and includes data-informed decision-making, the AI project lifecycle, and ethical questions such as bias and fairness. [3][4]

This direction is broader than teaching children to write prompts. Logical reasoning, problem decomposition, pattern recognition, data literacy and ethical decision-making can help learners understand both what a digital tool can do and what it cannot establish.

The existence of a curriculum and handbooks, however, is evidence of published policy—not proof of how consistently schools are delivering it, how much classroom time it receives, or whether students’ verification skills improve. The available BaSE figures do not evaluate the impact of CBSE’s 2026–27 curriculum.

UNESCO’s guidance on generative AI in education likewise stresses human-centred, age-appropriate use, data privacy and the need for educational institutions to validate tools rather than adopt them uncritically. This is an international framework for considering responsible implementation, not evidence that any specific Indian school is succeeding or failing. [5]

## What the survey says about benefits—and what it cannot prove

BaSE reports that 84% of respondents perceived improved learning from GenAI use. That finding matters as a record of users’ experience. But perceived improvement is not the same as a measured increase in test scores, retention or the ability to apply a concept later. The survey does not, in this result, isolate AI as the cause of an independently measured learning gain. [1]

That limitation should not be used to dismiss the benefit students report. It should clarify the next question. Does AI help a student understand a difficult concept and then solve a new problem independently? Does it simply make an answer faster to obtain? Under what conditions does the tool help, and when does it mislead?

Answering those questions requires different evidence: appropriately designed learning assessments, classroom observation, student and teacher interviews, and transparent tests of selected AI outputs. A newsroom test of model responses could be informative only if it published the prompts, model versions, dates, scoring rubric and limitations. A small set of examples would not establish how all AI systems perform or prove that AI causes better or worse learning.

## Access is not the same as equal opportunity

The report also provides a reminder that students do not necessarily use technology under the same conditions. It says 72% of children in surveyed households had access to a smartphone, while 68% had shared access and 4% had dedicated access. Those figures refer to children in the surveyed households; they are not universal estimates for every child in India. [1]

A lesson that assumes each student has a private device, a reliable connection and individual time with an AI tool may not fit the circumstances of every learner. Schools need ways to teach checking and responsible use even when access is shared, connectivity is intermittent, or students work in different languages.

This is also why a platform-neutral curriculum matters. Students should learn a method for checking information that works across changing products, rather than memorising the interface of one chatbot.

## The next test should measure judgement, not just adoption

The evidence supports a balanced conclusion. Generative AI is already part of learning routines for a meaningful share of the children using educational technology in the surveyed settings. The survey also records a difference between self-reported understanding and how some respondents characterise the technology. It does not show whether they can recognise fabricated sources, independently check answers, or retain more knowledge after using a tool.

The next phase of AI literacy should be judged by what students can demonstrate: whether they can verify a factual claim against a reliable source, identify uncertainty, explain an answer in their own words, protect personal information, and complete a related task without relying on the generated response.

Those outcomes should be measured, not assumed. The BaSE findings establish a baseline for reported access and use; CBSE’s published curriculum describes an educational response. The missing link is evidence about implementation and what students actually learn.

For schools, the practical goal is not to teach children to trust or distrust AI in the abstract. It is to teach them how to question a specific answer, test it against evidence and make a reasoned decision about whether it is useful.

## Method and limitations

This is a document-led explainer based on published CSF/BaSE and CBSE materials, the Government of India’s curriculum-launch notice, and UNESCO guidance. The Breakdown has not conducted independent student or teacher interviews for this draft, has not contacted CSF or CBSE for comment, and has not conducted an original AI-model benchmark. Accordingly, this story makes no claim about classroom implementation, product rankings, or measured learning gains. Any later findings from interviews or tests should be added only after verification and review.
