## 1. Editorial thesis and scope

The central question is not whether firecrackers contribute to air pollution; that relationship is well established. The unresolved public-accountability question is whether the Delhi-NCR regulatory experiment can reliably distinguish certified products from conventional or unauthorised ones, enforce restrictions on where and when sales and use occur, and generate comparable environmental and health evidence.

The 29 October 2026 Supreme Court order creates a detailed compliance framework. On 3 November, the Court took note of a Hindustan Times report alleging open sale of different types of firecrackers in Delhi markets and asked the law officer appearing for the Centre, Delhi government and CAQM to look into it. That is a meaningful new development, but the newspaper's observations are not The Breakdown's independent field findings.

This package therefore uses a document-led approach. It does not claim that The Breakdown visited markets, scanned products, interviewed officials, or independently audited hospital records. Those tasks are explicitly listed as pre-publication reporting gaps.

### What this story can establish now

- The exact requirements in the Supreme Court's 29 October order.
- The Court's stated concern that previous orders were not effectively implemented and that the 2025 trial did not produce the requested evidence.
- The official pollution baseline and GRAP response recorded for 2 November.
- The technical definition of green crackers published by CSIR-NEERI, including the minimum emission-reduction criteria.
- A potentially important implementation question: CSIR-NEERI's 2025 press release says it issues registration letters and does not provide QR/barcodes on products, while the 2026 Supreme Court order requires product-specific, non-transferable QR codes on each box. The identity of the QR-code issuer and the authoritative verification endpoint must be clarified, not guessed.
- The limits of using AQI movements alone to attribute pollution to fireworks.

### What this story cannot establish yet

- Whether the market allegations in the 3 November Hindustan Times report are accurate in every named location.
- Whether QR codes on actual products resolve to valid, product- and batch-specific records.
- Whether district administrations have published complete designated-market lists and no-cracker-zone maps.
- Whether police patrols, seizures, prosecutions and security-deposit rules have been implemented.
- Whether the 2026 experiment caused a measurable increase in hospital admissions or pollution relative to a defensible counterfactual.

# 2. Article draft

## Delhi-NCR's Green-Cracker Test Has a Familiar Weak Link: Enforcement

*The Supreme Court has ordered product-specific QR checks, designated sales points, police patrols and pollution and health monitoring for Diwali. But the Court's own record says earlier experiments failed to produce the evidence needed to judge the policy.*

On 3 November, the Supreme Court was confronted with an awkward question before the first permitted sales day for green firecrackers had even begun: could the authorities keep unauthorised products out of Delhi's markets?

The bench of Justices M.M. Sundresh and Prasanna B. Varale referred to a Hindustan Times report describing firecrackers being openly displayed and sold in several Delhi markets. The report said some vendors offered conventional crackers on request and that some products carried no QR code or codes that did not lead to verifiable certification details. The Court asked the Additional Solicitor General appearing for the Centre, Delhi government and the Commission for Air Quality Management to look into the allegations.

The Court did not conduct a market inspection or independently certify the newspaper's findings. But its intervention exposed the central test facing this year's temporary relaxation: a regulatory system can specify what is permitted on paper and still fail if products, sellers and locations cannot be verified on the ground.

The Court's 29 October order in *Arjun Gopal v. Union of India* (2026 INSC 1181) permits only specified, certified green firecrackers in Delhi-NCR for a tightly limited period. Eligible manufacturers may manufacture listed products between 2 and 6 November. Sales by eligible traders are permitted from 4 to 6 November. Bursting is allowed only between 6 am and 7 am, and between 8 pm and 10 pm, on 7 and 8 November. The complete ban is to return from 9 November.

The order also prohibits bursting within 300 metres of hospitals, nursing homes, primary and district health centres, old-age homes, notified silence zones and educational institutions. Sales are restricted to locations designated by district authorities; online sales are prohibited. Product boxes must be sealed and carry product-specific, non-transferable QR codes. The codes are supposed to disclose the manufacturer, NEERI registration, PESO or District Magistrate licence, formulation and test certificate.

These are not merely consumer-information instructions. They are the control system on which the temporary relaxation depends.

### A QR code is not proof unless it resolves to an authoritative record

The Court's order makes the QR code a key part of product verification. Police patrol teams are directed to check codes at random, confiscate illegal or conventional crackers and initiate prosecutions against offending manufacturers or traders.

But there is a technical question that deserves a direct answer from the institutions responsible for certification.

In a press release dated 16 October 2025, CSIR-NEERI stated that it issues registration letters for green crackers and does not provide QR codes or barcodes on products. Its current National Centre of Excellence information describes a product-specific registration process and says that NEERI-issued registration certificates and test reports available through its official online system are the authentic documents.

That does not, by itself, establish a contradiction with the 2026 court order. The order requires a QR code on each box; it does not say in the quoted direction that NEERI itself must generate that code. Manufacturers or another authorised system may be responsible for encoding the information. But the public needs to know who issues the code, what database it points to, how a consumer or police officer can authenticate the record, and how a copied or recycled code is detected.

A QR code that opens a product catalogue is not the same as a QR code that verifies a specific batch against an authoritative registration and test record. The distinction is central to the Court's requirement that codes be product-specific and non-transferable.

The question is therefore not simply whether a box has a QR symbol. It is whether the scan produces a verifiable chain linking the physical product and batch to the manufacturer, registration, licence and test certificate.

### 'Green' does not mean pollution-free

CSIR-NEERI's published definition is more precise than the marketing language sometimes used around green crackers. It describes green formulations as products designed to reduce emissions compared with the conventional composition in the same category. Its stated criteria include a minimum 30% reduction in particulate matter, or a minimum 20% reduction in particulate matter together with at least a 10% reduction in gaseous emissions.

NEERI's page also identifies particulate matter, sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides and metals among the pollutants associated with fireworks. It describes laboratory testing for particulate and gaseous emissions, noise and banned chemicals.

The comparison matters. A certified product is intended to emit less than its conventional comparator under the relevant test conditions. That is not a claim that it emits nothing, nor does a laboratory reduction percentage establish the total pollution burden when many products are used across a large urban region.

The Court's order also prohibits barium-containing products, firecrackers not approved by CSIR-NEERI and joined firecracker strings commonly called 'laris'. The order requires manufacturers to use only products uploaded to the NEERI website during the specified period and requires sealed boxes to prevent mixing with unauthorised products.

Those safeguards make product identity and batch traceability more than administrative details. If a certified label can be placed on an uncertified product, or if a code cannot be tied to a batch, the claimed emissions reduction cannot be assumed for the item being sold.

### The Court says the evidence from the previous experiment did not arrive

The present order is also a response to a longer failure of measurement.

The Court's 29 October judgment records that a temporary relaxation in 2025 was intended to let authorities determine the environmental and health effects of allowing green crackers and report the findings. The Court said the States had failed to comply with those directions.

It also said it did not have adequate data to decide whether the complete ban imposed in 2024 had served its purpose. The judgment noted that air-quality readings had not shown significant improvement during the period under discussion and referred to a rise in AQI around Dussehra despite a complete ban.

That is not proof that a ban has no effect. Nor does a pollution spike during a ban prove that firecrackers were not a contributor. The Court's point is narrower and more important: without reliable compliance records and comparable environmental and health data, it cannot properly evaluate which regulatory approach works.

The new order attempts to close that gap. The Central Pollution Control Board, in consultation with State Pollution Control Boards and regional offices, must prepare daily pre-Diwali AQI reports for 2–6 November, Diwali reports for 7–8 November and post-Diwali reports for 9–30 November. Authorities must also collect sand and water samples for comparative analysis and inspect monitoring stations alleged to be non-functional.

The States must submit an affidavit stating the precise increase in patient admissions in hospitals and health centres across Delhi-NCR through 30 November following the festival. Compliance reports and affidavits are due by 14 December, with the matter listed for further hearing on 16 December.

The value of this exercise will depend on the quality of the underlying records: which monitoring stations were functioning, what pollutants were measured, how missing observations were treated, how hospitals defined relevant admissions, and whether the data can distinguish a short-lived fireworks episode from the region's wider pollution burden.

### Delhi's pollution baseline is already poor

The pollution context is serious before the permitted firecracker-use window.

CAQM's 2 November press release records Delhi's daily average AQI at 324, in the 'Very Poor' category, and says Stage II of the Graded Response Action Plan was invoked across the NCR with immediate effect. The Commission attributed the rise to unfavourable meteorological conditions and said the forecast expected very poor air quality to continue.

The Ministry of Earth Sciences' IITM air-quality early-warning bulletin dated 3 November also recorded the 2 November 4 pm CPCB AQI of 324. It forecast very poor conditions from 3 to 6 November and a subsequent six-day outlook ranging from very poor to severe.

Station-level data should not be confused with the city average. IITM's station dashboard recorded Jahangirpuri at 452 at 9 am on 3 November, while readings at other stations varied. A single station's severe reading is important local evidence, but it is not Delhi's daily average.

This distinction will matter after Diwali. If the city average rises, that alone will not establish how much of the increase came from fireworks. Weather, wind, mixing depth, transport emissions, dust, industrial activity and regional biomass burning can all affect measured concentrations.

A peer-reviewed 2024 study of Delhi's Diwali pollution, analysing 2019, 2020 and 2021, used source-apportionment methods and reported that fireworks accounted for more than 85% of the *elemental mass* measured in PM2.5 during the Diwali period. The authors also discussed the overlap with biomass-burning activity and used meteorological normalisation. The finding is relevant evidence that fireworks can be a major source of fireworks-related elemental aerosols during Diwali. It is not an estimate of the share of all PM2.5 in 2026, nor a direct evaluation of the currently certified green-cracker formulations.

A defensible assessment needs pollutant composition, time-resolved monitoring, meteorological context and a transparent comparison period—not just a before-and-after AQI headline.

### The accountability test has four links

The 2026 order can be evaluated through four observable links.

**First: product verification.** Does every permitted box carry a working, product- and batch-specific code? Does the scan resolve to an authoritative registration and test certificate? Can a copied code be identified?

**Second: market control.** Have district administrations published the designated sale locations? Are licences and security deposits recorded? Are sales outside those sites, online orders and unauthorised products being detected and acted upon?

**Third: enforcement.** Are police patrols operating at the designated sites? How many inspections, QR checks, seizures, prosecutions and licence cancellations are recorded? Are those records published in a form that can be checked?

**Fourth: environmental and health measurement.** Are AQI stations working and reporting consistently? Are sand and water samples collected with a documented method? Do hospitals use a consistent definition for the admission data requested by the Court? Can the resulting reports distinguish correlation from causation?

The Supreme Court has ordered authorities to provide much of this information. The reporting deadline is still ahead. Until the records are filed and examined, claims that the experiment has succeeded or failed would be premature.

### The policy argument is not settled by a single AQI number

There is a serious counterargument to a blanket ban. In its judgment, the Court noted that a complete prohibition had not prevented the sale of conventional crackers and that illegal products were reportedly being sold under the guise of green crackers. It reasoned that a regulated route for tested products might reduce the incentive for illegal trade while allowing a limited, measurable experiment.

That argument deserves examination rather than dismissal. But it depends on the controls working. If certification cannot be checked at the point of sale, designated markets are not enforced, or the environmental and health data are incomplete, the experiment cannot answer the question it was designed to address.

The Court has set a reporting deadline. The public should be able to see the same evidence that will be placed before it.

For now, the most important measure of the green-cracker experiment is not the label on a box or the AQI on a single morning. It is whether authorities can produce a verifiable record connecting certified products, lawful sales, enforcement actions, pollution measurements and health outcomes.

That is the evidence Delhi-NCR has been asked to produce. The test has begun; the proof has not yet arrived.

Request written records from Delhi, Gurugram, Faridabad, Noida and Ghaziabad district administrations and police:

- Designated sale-location notifications and maps.
- Trader licence lists and temporary licence restoration orders.
- Security-deposit amounts and forfeiture conditions.
- Patrol-team orders, inspection schedules and responsible units.
- Seizure, prosecution, licence-cancellation and complaint registers.
- Action taken after the 3 November Supreme Court observation.

Do not purchase, transport, handle or test explosive products. ### C. Pollution monitoring and health data

**Questions to CPCB / DPCC / NCR State Pollution Control Boards**
