# Review draft — BCCI's domestic mandate and the economics of a Ranji season

**Status:** Editorial review required; not approved for publication.  
**Working thesis:** A domestic participation directive is also an economic and governance intervention: it changes the opportunity cost of a player's time, injury risk and contract choices. But assessing its effect requires separating BCCI rules, state-association payments, central retainers, Test incentives and franchise contracts—and testing any participation claim against match-level data.  
**Target:** 1,800–2,600 narrative words.  
**Current production baseline:** 320 narrative words; 5 paragraphs; declared 10-minute read.

## Proposed opening

For a professional cricketer, choosing between domestic first-class cricket and a franchise opportunity is not simply a question of loyalty. It can involve guaranteed fees, contract terms, injury exposure, selection consequences and the uncertain value of future international appearances. A policy that requires more domestic participation therefore has both a sporting rationale and an economic dimension.

The public debate often mixes several different payment systems. A Ranji match fee is not a central BCCI retainer. A Test match fee is not the same as the Test Cricket Incentive Scheme. An IPL contract is not comparable to a state-association payment without accounting for availability, selection and contract conditions. The economics only becomes clear when those categories are separated.

## Reporting sequence

1. **Reconstruct the directive.** Obtain the original BCCI communication, its date, eligibility rules, exemptions, medical exceptions and the exact players covered. Distinguish formal policy from public statements and media interpretations.
2. **Explain selection incentives.** Establish what domestic participation does and does not guarantee about national selection. Avoid treating correlation as proof that a directive caused a selection outcome.
3. **Build a fee ledger.** Use dated BCCI documents for domestic match fees and Test match fees; verify whether later revisions exist. State the season, playing-day assumptions and whether the figure applies to the playing XI or reserves.
4. **Explain the Test incentive.** Quote the actual qualifying condition and separate playing-XI and non-playing-XI rates. Do not summarize the schedule as one flat rate for all players.
5. **Compare income sources carefully.** Central retainers, match fees, incentives, state-level benefits and franchise contracts have different eligibility, duration and certainty. Do not estimate annual earnings without player-specific contracts and participation data.
6. **Consider the player perspective.** Report the costs of travel, training, injury and foregone opportunities only with attributable interviews or documented evidence. Do not invent anonymous players or quotes.
7. **Consider the board and team perspective.** Test the argument that domestic cricket maintains match fitness, protects the depth of India's first-class pool and creates a credible pathway for selection. Seek evidence from selection patterns and named experts.
8. **Measure participation.** Define the player cohort (India-capped, India A, centrally contracted, or another reproducible group), comparison seasons, appearances, matches available and exclusions. Use scorecards and published squads; publish the dataset and method.
9. **Counterevidence and limits.** Availability can be affected by injury, workload management, national duty and selection decisions. A simple before/after total may misattribute those changes to the directive.
10. **Conclusion.** The policy's success should be evaluated against transparent participation data and the economics players actually face, not a single headline percentage.

## Editorial critique before revision

The current draft makes a strong causal-sounding appearance claim without showing a reproducible dataset and blurs the Test incentive tiers. It needs primary policy documents, a transparent match-level method, and reporting from both players and administrators. It must not present fee schedules as total compensation or imply that one season's appearances prove the policy's effect.
