# Review draft — AI literacy in Indian schools

**Status:** Editorial review required; not approved for publication.  
**Working thesis:** The important question is not whether Indian students have encountered AI tools, but whether schools teach them to test an AI-generated answer against evidence—and whether available evidence measures that ability at all.  
**Target:** 1,800–2,400 narrative words.  
**Current production baseline:** 311 narrative words; 6 paragraphs; declared 8-minute read.

## Proposed opening

A convincing answer from an AI system can look like knowledge. It can also contain a false claim, an invented citation or a fact that was once true but is no longer current. The practical test of AI literacy is therefore not whether a student can prompt a chatbot. It is whether the student can identify a checkable claim, find the underlying evidence, compare the two and explain what remains uncertain.

That distinction should shape any account of AI literacy in Indian schools. Policy documents can establish what a curriculum asks teachers to cover. Surveys can describe reported access, attitudes or use. Neither, on its own, establishes that students can reliably verify an AI-generated answer in practice.

## Reporting sequence and evidence requirements

1. **Define the skill.** Distinguish basic digital familiarity, AI use, knowledge of model limitations, source evaluation and demonstrated verification. Do not use them interchangeably.
2. **What the curriculum asks for.** Review the applicable CBSE 2026–27 Computational Thinking and AI curriculum and resource books. Quote only from the documents themselves and explain their scope; curriculum publication is not evidence of implementation in every school.
3. **What surveys measure.** Obtain the original BaSE 2025 report and methodology. Verify the reported 12,500-household and 2,500-teacher sample, ten-state coverage, fieldwork period, exact questions and weighting. Do not imply that the teacher sample is the same as a student skills assessment.
4. **What a real verification task requires.** Explain the difference between asking a model for an answer and opening its cited primary document. Demonstrate the process only with a real, checked example whose underlying claim and sources have been independently verified.
5. **The teacher's constraint.** Report evidence about teacher preparation, time, device access, connectivity and language. Separate documented findings from hypotheses; interview teachers and obtain permission for attributable quotations.
6. **Unequal conditions.** Compare what is feasible in different school contexts only when comparable data exist. Avoid a simplistic urban/rural binary.
7. **The strongest counterargument.** AI tools may help students explore concepts, translate material or receive feedback. The question is how verification and teacher oversight are taught, not whether every use of AI is inherently harmful.
8. **What success would look like.** Propose a transparent assessment: students identify a factual claim, trace a citation, open a primary source, decide whether the evidence supports the claim and state a limitation. Label this as a proposed assessment unless evaluated data exists.
9. **Limitations and conclusion.** State what the available surveys do not measure and what evidence would be needed to assess learning outcomes.

## Editorial critique before revision

The current article's main weakness is that it risks treating policy, awareness and competence as the same thing. The next revision must add the original survey's methodology, actual curriculum passages, a verified worked example, teacher-side constraints and explicit limits. Do not write fabricated classroom scenes, invent teacher quotes or present the proposed assessment as an evaluated intervention.

## Release blockers

- Full primary-source review of the BaSE report and CBSE documents.
- Reporting on implementation and teacher experience; no interviews or quotes may be invented.
