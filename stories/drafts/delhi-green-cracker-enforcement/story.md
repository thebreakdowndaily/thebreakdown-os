# Green Crackers Are Not Pollution-Free. Delhi's Diwali Test Must Prove More Than a QR Code

## The Supreme Court has created a two-day window for certified products. The experiment will be meaningful only if product identity, enforcement and environmental measurements can all be independently checked.

**The Breakdown | Investigation | 3 November 2026**

> **Editorial status: DRAFT — NOT CLEARED FOR PUBLICATION.** This is desk research. The Breakdown has not conducted physical market inspections, scanned retail products, obtained police seizure records, interviewed officials or acquired the complete station-level pollution and hospital datasets.

## THE BOTTOM LINE

The Supreme Court has temporarily permitted the manufacture and sale of specified certified green firecrackers in Delhi-NCR and allowed their use on November 7 and 8, 2026, within two daily time windows. The complete prohibition is to return from November 9. The order requires sealed boxes, product- and batch-specific QR codes, designated sales locations, licensed traders, police patrols, random QR checks, confiscations and prosecutions. It also orders daily air-quality reports, environmental sampling and hospital-admission reporting. [1]

But three questions remain distinct. Can a buyer or enforcement officer establish that a particular box corresponds to an actual CSIR-NEERI registration and the relevant PESO or District Magistrate licence? Will authorities carry out and document the inspections, seizures and prosecutions required by the Court? Will environmental and health data be adequate to assess the consequences of the permitted use?

A green-cracker label is not proof that a product is pollution-free. CSIR-NEERI's published criteria define green crackers by reductions in specified emissions compared with conventional crackers in the same category. The criteria include a minimum 30 per cent reduction in particulate matter, or a minimum 20 per cent reduction in particulate matter together with at least a 10 per cent reduction in gaseous emissions. The products still emit pollutants. [2]

The test is not whether a box carries a green label. It is whether product certification, batch traceability, enforcement records and environmental measurements can be connected into one verifiable evidence chain.

## WHAT THE COURT HAS ORDERED

In Arjun Gopal and Others v. Union of India and Others, 2026 INSC 1181, the Supreme Court issued directions on October 29, 2026. The order was digitally signed on November 2 and made public that day. [1]

The permitted use is limited to November 7 and 8, between 6 am and 7 am and between 8 pm and 10 pm. Bursting is prohibited within 300 metres of hospitals, nursing homes, primary and district health centres, old-age homes, notified silence zones and educational institutions. The relevant areas are to be declared and marked as no-cracker zones. [1]

The order sets out an operational sequence:

- Manufacturers with valid CSIR-NEERI certification and PESO authorisation may manufacture only products uploaded on the NEERI website between November 2 and 6.
- Boxes must be sealed so approved products are not mixed with unauthorised or conventional firecrackers.
- Only licensed traders may sell approved products, and only at designated locations.
- Trader licences cancelled earlier are to be restored temporarily from November 4 to 6, subject to compliance.
- Online sales are prohibited.
- Firecrackers containing barium, products not approved as green crackers, and firecrackers joined in series (laris) or garlands are prohibited under the October 29 directions.
- Authorities must check vehicles entering NCR to prevent the movement of illegal and conventional firecrackers.
- Police and district-administration patrol teams must conduct inspections and random QR-code checks, confiscate prohibited products and initiate prosecutions. [1]

The Court also directed manufacturers and traders to furnish monetary security, with forfeiture in the event of violations. District authorities must publish designated sale locations and display information explaining how consumers can identify approved products and report violations. [1]

These are requirements. They are not evidence that the requirements have been implemented.

## THE QR-CODE QUESTION: WHO ISSUES IT, AND WHAT DOES IT PROVE?

The QR code is central to the system because the Court requires it to identify a particular product and batch. Scanning the code should disclose the manufacturer, NEERI registration, PESO or District Magistrate licence, formulation and test certificate. The code must be non-transferable. [1]

There is an important detail in CSIR-NEERI's own public explanation.

In an October 16, 2025 press release, NEERI said it issues registration letters for green firecrackers but does not provide QR codes or barcodes on products. It said manufacturers develop and display product-specific QR codes, which include regulatory details such as NEERI registration and the PESO licence. The institute said regulatory agencies such as PESO and CPCB verify those codes. [3]

This does not, by itself, establish a contradiction with the 2026 Court order. The order requires a product- and batch-specific code and describes the information it must reveal; it also records the requirement that manufacturers print the code on packaging. NEERI's statement clarifies that the institute's registration letter and the manufacturer's QR code are separate parts of the traceability system.

That distinction matters to consumers. A QR code is not an NEERI certificate merely because it displays an NEERI registration number or logo. The code must resolve to information that matches the actual manufacturer, product, batch and relevant authorisations. A generic product catalogue, a copied code or a code that cannot be checked against an authoritative record would not establish that the box is genuine.

NEERI's public green-firecracker page says registration certificates and test reports issued through its official online website are the only authentic and valid documents. It warns against fake registration certificates and test reports. [2]

The public-facing verification question is specific: can the code on a physical box be matched to the NEERI registration and test record, the relevant PESO or district licence, and the product and batch in the box?

The Breakdown has not physically scanned a product or independently tested the live QR-validation process. We therefore cannot say that a particular code is genuine or fake.

## WHAT “GREEN” MEANS — AND WHAT IT DOES NOT

CSIR-NEERI's published definition does not describe green crackers as pollution-free.

Its criteria compare a product with a conventional cracker in the same category. A product can qualify through at least 30 per cent particulate-matter reduction, or through at least 20 per cent particulate-matter reduction combined with at least 10 per cent reduction in gaseous emissions. NEERI identifies particulate matter, sulphur dioxide and nitrogen dioxide among the relevant emissions. [2]

NEERI describes a dedicated testing arrangement for particulate and gaseous emissions and a separate noise-testing facility. Its published account says particulate matter is measured gravimetrically and dissolved gases are measured spectrophotometrically; the air-capture and measurement procedures follow CPCB-prescribed methods. Noise measurement uses a Type 1 integrating sound-level meter under specified environmental conditions. [2]

The institute's October 2025 press release reported that reductions of 30 to 50 per cent had been achieved in some formulations. It also described testing for particulate matter, gaseous pollutants, noise, raw-material purity and banned chemicals. Those are institute-reported test and development results, not a measurement of the total pollution generated across Delhi-NCR during Diwali. [3]

The distinction is fundamental:

1. **Product test:** measures emissions from a tested product under specified laboratory conditions.
2. **Market compliance:** establishes whether the product being sold is the product that was tested and registered.
3. **Ambient air quality:** measures pollutants in the surrounding atmosphere, where firecrackers are only one of several possible sources.
4. **Health impact:** requires evidence beyond a simple change in AQI or hospital admissions.

A percentage reduction in emissions from a tested product cannot be translated directly into the same percentage reduction in Delhi's ambient AQI. The city-level result also depends on how many products are used, their composition and quantity, where and when they are used, weather and other pollution sources.

## THE AIR-QUALITY BASELINE IS ALREADY COMPLEX

The Ministry of Earth Sciences' Air Quality Early Warning System, operated by the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology, reported that Delhi's CPCB AQI was 324 at 4 pm on November 2, placing it in the Very Poor category. Its November 3 bulletin also described meteorological conditions and forecasts for the following days. [4]

Separately, the Commission for Air Quality Management invoked Stage II of the Graded Response Action Plan across NCR on November 2 after Delhi's AQI reached the Very Poor category. News reports quoting the CAQM order recorded a rise from 117 on October 31 to 261 on November 1 and 324 on November 2, with unfavourable meteorological conditions cited in the order. [5]

The baseline matters, but it does not identify the source of pollution.

Delhi's official AQI portal explains that its hourly city AQI is the average of readings from stations reporting during that hour. Stations without a reading in the preceding two hours are excluded from the average and marked as not reporting. The portal also says real-time values are provisional and may change after CPCB quality checks. [6]

That methodology has direct implications for the Court-ordered comparison. A station reading, an hourly city average, a daily AQI figure and a pollutant concentration are not interchangeable. A credible assessment must retain the station, pollutant, timestamp, averaging period and data-quality status.

The Court has directed the CPCB, in consultation with State Pollution Control Boards and regional offices in NCR, to prepare daily pre-Diwali reports for November 2–6, Diwali reports for November 7–8 and post-Diwali reports for November 9–30. It has also directed collection of sand and water samples for comparative analysis. [1]

A before-and-after AQI comparison alone cannot isolate the contribution of firecrackers. Weather, wind speed, temperature inversion, regional transport, road dust, construction, industrial emissions, traffic and agricultural burning can all influence observed pollution. The IITM bulletin itself includes meteorological conditions alongside its air-quality observations. [4]

For that reason, reporting must obtain the underlying station-level readings and the methods used to select and analyse environmental samples. Without them, the data may show that air quality changed, but not why.

## THE PREVIOUS EXPERIMENT LEFT A DOCUMENTED EVIDENCE GAP

The October 29 order looks back at the Court's October 15, 2025 relaxation. The Court said one purpose of the earlier temporary relaxation was to enable States to determine the environmental and health effects of allowing green crackers and place findings before the Court. It then recorded that the States had not complied with the directions to the Court's satisfaction. [1]

The Court also said conventional firecrackers continued to be sold under the guise of green crackers despite the complete ban. It described non-implementation and flouting of earlier directions as a recurring problem. [1]

Those are judicial observations. They should be reported as the Court's findings, not converted into a claim that every authority failed or that every market sold illegal products.

The Court's October 2026 order attempts to create a more detailed implementation record: designated sale locations, patrol teams, QR checks, random sampling, confiscations, prosecutions, environmental reports and health data. The relevant authorities must file affidavits and reports by December 14, 2026. The matter is listed for further compliance on December 16. [1]

The 2026 exercise will be assessable only if those records are complete, comparable and accessible.

## WHAT THE PUBLIC SHOULD BE ABLE TO VERIFY

A meaningful public record would answer at least six questions.

**1. Where were sales legally permitted?** Publish designated-location lists by district, the notification date, the responsible authority and the temporary trader licences restored for November 4–6.

**2. Which products were eligible?** Publish or preserve the NEERI product list made available for the November 2–6 window, with manufacturer, product, formulation, registration and batch identifiers. A general manufacturer register is not a substitute for the product-and-batch record required by the Court.

**3. Did the QR system work?** Report how many boxes were checked, how many codes resolved to matching records, how many failed or were duplicated, and how discrepancies were investigated. Publish a safe verification method that does not expose commercially sensitive or security-sensitive information.

**4. What did enforcement produce?** Report inspections, vehicle checks, seizures, prosecutions, licence cancellations and forfeited security deposits, with dates and districts. A patrol order or press statement is not a substitute for these records.

**5. What happened to air quality?** Publish station-level hourly data and daily reports, identify missing readings and quality-control adjustments, and explain how weather and other pollution sources were considered. Publish the locations, collection times, laboratory methods and results for sand and water samples.

**6. What happened to health indicators?** Publish aggregate hospital and health-centre admission data with clear definitions, comparable baseline periods and coverage. A change in admissions cannot automatically be attributed to fireworks without a defensible analysis.

The Court has directed the authorities to report the exact increase in patient admissions across Delhi-NCR hospitals and health centres through November 30 following the festival. The reports are due by December 14. [1]

## WHAT WE CAN AND CANNOT CONCLUDE NOW

The legal framework is documented. The product-certification criteria and NEERI's explanation of the QR system are publicly available. The official early-warning bulletin establishes that Delhi was already experiencing Very Poor air quality on November 2. The Court has itself identified a prior evidence gap and ordered a more detailed reporting exercise. [1–6]

But as of November 3, this desk research has not independently established whether designated markets have been notified in every NCR district, whether the live 2026 product-and-batch list is complete, whether QR scans reliably resolve to matching certification records, or how many inspections and seizures have taken place.

Nor is there yet post-event air-quality or health evidence for the November 7–8 permitted-use period.

The appropriate conclusion is limited: the Court has created a testable framework, but the success of its implementation and the environmental consequences remain to be demonstrated through records and independent verification.

The next stage is to obtain designated-market notifications, the current product-and-batch register, enforcement records and the underlying environmental and health datasets. Those documents will determine whether the story can move from a desk-based investigation to a fully reported account.
