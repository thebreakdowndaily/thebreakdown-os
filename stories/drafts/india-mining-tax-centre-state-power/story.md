# Who lost the power to tax India's minerals?

## The August 2026 mining law that reshapes Centre–state fiscal power

**Deck:** Parliament passed a law on August 13, 2026, that reverses the fiscal gain the Supreme Court gave mineral-rich states in 2024. Jharkhand says it stands to lose ₹8,000–10,000 crore a year. The Centre says it is about uniformity. The real question is whether this is mining reform or a federal power grab.

**The Breakdown** | August 18, 2026

---

## THE BOTTOM LINE

The MMDR Amendment Act, 2026 does not strip states of all mineral-taxation power. It makes every state tax, cess, or levy on mineral rights and mineral-bearing land subject to conditions prescribed by the Central government. In practice, this means the Centre now holds veto power over what mineral-rich states can charge mining companies. For Jharkhand — which budgeted ₹14,656 crore from its Mineral Bearing Land Cess in 2026–27 — the fiscal exposure is large and real. The law's constitutional validity is almost certainly heading to court.

---

## WHAT HAPPENED?

On August 13, 2026, the Rajya Sabha passed the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Amendment Bill, 2026, completing the parliamentary process. The Lok Sabha had approved it on August 12. The Bill was introduced on August 10 — three days before passage — and awaits Presidential assent.

The legislation amends the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957 (MMDR Act), the principal law governing India's mining sector. It inserts a new Section 9D that prohibits states from levying "any tax, cess or such other levy" on mineral rights or mineral-bearing lands, except in accordance with conditions or restrictions prescribed by the Central government. It expands the Union's regulatory reach to cover mineral-bearing land itself — not just mines and mineral development. And it invalidates any unpaid or unrecovered dues from state-level mineral levies imposed before the Amendment Act comes into force, while protecting amounts already collected.

Coal and Mines Minister G Kishan Reddy told Parliament that the objective was not to increase the Centre's revenue from mining but to "boost domestic mineral production, reduce import dependence and create jobs." He said states would retain full control over 49 minor minerals and that the changes affected only 11 to 12 major-mineral-producing states.

## WHAT ACTUALLY CHANGED?

The constitutional architecture of mineral taxation in India rests on several provisions in the Seventh Schedule of the Constitution. Here is what the law allowed, what states did, what the Supreme Court said, and what Parliament changed.

**What the Constitution allows:**

The Constitution splits mineral-related powers between the Centre and states across four entries:

- **Entry 54 (Union List):** Parliament can regulate mines and mineral development — but only if it passes a law declaring this "expedient in the public interest."
- **Entry 23 (State List):** States can regulate mines and mineral development, but this is subject to Entry 54.
- **Entry 18 (State List):** Land — including rights in land — is a state subject. The Centre has no general power over land.
- **Entry 49 (State List):** States can tax lands and buildings.
- **Entry 50 (State List):** States can tax mineral rights, but "subject to any limitations imposed by Parliament by law relating to mineral development."

In plain terms: the Constitution gives states the primary power over land and mineral taxation, while giving Parliament the power to regulate mining activity and to limit state mineral taxes if Parliament chooses to pass a law doing so.

**What states historically did:**

For 35 years after the 1989 *India Cement* Supreme Court judgment, the prevailing legal position was that royalty was a tax — and since royalty was the only levy permitted under the MMDR framework, states could not impose anything on top of it.

**What the Supreme Court said in 2024:**

On July 25, 2024, a nine-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court dismantled that position. In *Mineral Area Development Authority v. Steel Authority of India* (MADA), the Court ruled 8:1 that:

1. **Royalty is not a tax.** It is a contractual payment for the right to extract minerals, not a government levy imposed by law.
2. **States retain the power to tax mineral rights** under Entry 50 and to tax mineral-bearing land under Entry 49.
3. **The MMDR Act does not restrict** state taxation — it is silent on the matter.
4. **Parliament can restrict** Entry 50 taxation through a future mineral-development law, but this power does not extend to Entry 49 (taxing land). Parliament cannot limit states' power to tax mineral-bearing land.

The Court allowed states to raise tax demands from April 1, 2005, but staggered payments over 12 years from April 1, 2026. Review petitions were dismissed in October 2024.

**What Parliament changed in 2026:**

The 2026 Amendment exercises precisely the power the Supreme Court said Parliament had — but extends it further than the Court's framework contemplated:

- **New Section 9D** bars states from taxing mineral rights or mineral-bearing land except "in accordance with such conditions or restrictions as may be prescribed by the Central Government." This covers any levy based on mineral quantity, value, or royalty.
- **Amended Section 2** extends Central government control to "mineral-bearing lands" — defined as land with mineral content meeting prescribed parameters.
- **Amended Section 13** empowers the Centre to frame rules prescribing the conditions under which states may levy taxes — a significant delegation of legislative power to the executive.
- **Invalidation provision** deems unpaid or unrecovered pre-Amendment state levies invalid. Amounts already collected are not refundable.

In one move, the Centre converted the Supreme Court's conditional permission (Parliament *may* restrict Entry 50 taxation through law) into an executive gatekeeping power (the Centre *will* decide what states can charge, through rules it alone frames).

**Why the legal question now matters:**

The Supreme Court was precise: Parliament can restrict Entry 50 (mineral rights taxation) but not Entry 49 (land taxation). The 2026 Amendment attempts to restrict both — and extends Central control to mineral-bearing land, which is Entry 18 (land). Whether this survives judicial scrutiny is the central constitutional question.

## WHY THIS MATTERS

For an ordinary reader, the mechanism matters because it determines where money flows.

India's mining sector produces coal, iron ore, limestone, manganese, copper, and bauxite, among other minerals. The states where these minerals are found — Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Karnataka, Rajasthan — have historically relied on royalties, auction premiums, and, increasingly, state-level cesses to fund welfare programmes, infrastructure, and public services.

The 2024 Supreme Court judgment opened a fiscal door. Jharkhand moved first, passing the Jharkhand Mineral Bearing Land Cess Act in October 2024, initially at ₹100 per tonne on iron ore and later raised to ₹400 per tonne. Tamil Nadu imposed a similar cess at ₹160 per tonne on limestone. Karnataka proposed a tax on non-auctioned iron ore mines.

The 2026 Amendment effectively slams that door shut — or at minimum, puts the Centre's hand on the latch.

## WHO GAINS, WHO LOSES

**Who loses:**

- **Jharkhand** is the most exposed. Its 2026–27 Budget estimated ₹14,656 crore from the Mineral Bearing Land Cess. Chief Minister Hemant Soren wrote to Prime Minister Narendra Modi on August 13, 2026, stating that mining revenue accounted for approximately 84.9% of the state's own non-tax revenue in 2024–25. Soren estimated the legislation could result in an annual revenue loss of ₹8,000–10,000 crore, "directly affecting the state's ability to sustain developmental, welfare and social-security interventions." The JMM has put the broader figure at ₹15,000 crore annually when all mineral-related revenues are considered. The state has created a Budget Stabilisation Fund (₹832 crore in 2025–26, ₹1,209 crore budgeted for 2026–27) as a buffer — but this is modest relative to the potential loss.
- **Tamil Nadu** imposed an MBL tax of ₹160/tonne on limestone and will face similar restrictions.
- **Kerala** has announced formal opposition. Chief Minister V D Satheesan wrote to Prime Minister Modi on August 17, 2026, stating the law "transfers states' rights and authority over land to the jurisdiction of the Union government" and pledging to explore legal and political remedies. Kerala has not yet filed a formal legal challenge.
- **Karnataka** had proposed a similar tax and now faces the same constraint.

**Who gains:**

- **Mining companies** gain fiscal certainty. The tax landscape across states was becoming fragmented — different cesses, different rates, different timing. An expert cited by *The Indian Express* said the MBL tax was "the biggest additional burden on the industry." The invalidation of unpaid pre-Amendment dues — estimated at approximately ₹2 lakh crore across the sector (an industry estimate, not an official figure) — is a direct financial benefit to miners who had not yet paid.
- **The Centre** gains regulatory control without directly collecting mineral revenue. Mines Minister Reddy told Parliament that the Centre's mineral revenue has declined 23%, and that it does not receive mineral revenue apart from its 9% GST share. The Centre's stated position is that it wants to cap combined state levies at a reasonable percentage — to be determined after consultation with states. The PIB factsheet states that "approximately 90% of total mining taxes and statutory payments continue to accrue to the states."

## WHAT THE GOVERNMENT SAYS

The Centre's case rests on several arguments:

**Uniformity.** The Statement of Objects and Reasons of the Bill says "uneven imposition of taxes and other levies by states" has led to multiple taxes on mineral production, non-uniform rates across states, and retrospective impositions. The Centre argues this raises mining costs and discourages extraction.

**Investment certainty.** Mining industry representatives support the change. B K Bhatia, former Director General of the Federation of Indian Mineral Industries, told *The Indian Express* that the amendments would help prevent "multiple and excessive levies" and provide "greater certainty over the fiscal burden."

**States already receive the bulk.** Minister Reddy told Parliament that states' share of mining revenue has risen to 85% and their share of coal revenue to 96%. The PIB factsheet says states receive ~90% of all mining taxes and statutory payments. The Centre does not plan to increase its share.

**Minor minerals untouched.** The Centre stresses that 49 minor minerals remain under full state control.

**Existing levies continue.** A senior Mines Ministry official told *The Indian Express* that 14 existing levies in the mineral sector "will continue to be there" and that the Centre's concern is setting a ceiling — "all these levies together should not be beyond a certain percentage."

**Import dependence.** India imported minerals worth ₹10,12,529 crore in FY 2025–26. The Centre argues that a predictable, uniform fiscal framework is needed to encourage domestic production.

## WHAT THE CRITICS SAY

**Constitutional overreach.** PRS Legislative Research flagged that Parliament may lack legislative competence over mineral-bearing land. Land is Entry 18 (a state subject). The Supreme Court specifically held that Parliament's power to restrict state taxation operates on Entry 50 (mineral rights), not on Entry 49 (taxing land). The 2026 Amendment extends Central control to mineral-bearing land — which the Court's framework did not authorise.

**Retrospective invalidation.** The Bill deems pre-Amendment unpaid state levies invalid. PRS analysis notes this may violate the Supreme Court's 2024 directions, which permitted states to raise demands from April 2005. The 1993 *State of Haryana v. Karnal Co-Op Farmers' Society* precedent holds that Parliament can retrospectively change law to remove the basis of a judgment — but only by altering the legal basis, not by simply declaring the judgment ineffective. The Bill's approach may amount to "encroachment by the legislature upon the domain of the judiciary."

**Article 14 violation.** The Bill invalidates unpaid dues but does not refund amounts already collected. Mining companies that paid promptly are treated differently from those that delayed — a potential violation of the equality guarantee.

**Excessive delegation.** The Bill leaves the conditions and restrictions on state taxation entirely to Central government rules, with no guiding principles. Under the *Re Delhi Laws Act* (1951) doctrine, essential legislative functions cannot be delegated. PRS flagged this as potentially excessive delegation.

**Fiscal autonomy.** Jharkhand's CM argues that mineral revenues are "not marginal receipts but a critical component of the state's fiscal capacity." Kerala's CM framed the law as an "encroachment on states' constitutional authority." The core argument: states bear the environmental and social costs of mining (displacement, pollution, infrastructure damage) and should have the fiscal tools to compensate their populations.

**Political opposition spans parties.** The objection is not limited to opposition-ruled states. Jharkhand (JMM), Kerala (INC-led), and Karnataka (INC) span the political spectrum. Congress MP Manish Tewari argued the Bill should have been sent to a parliamentary committee given its implications for Centre–state power.

## WHAT THE EVIDENCE SHOWS

The factual picture is relatively clear. The constitutional question is not.

**On the facts:**

- States did gain new taxation power from the 2024 Supreme Court judgment. Jharkhand and Tamil Nadu exercised it. Karnataka proposed to.
- The 2026 Amendment restricts that power by making it conditional on Central government rules.
- The Centre does not directly collect mineral revenue; states receive the overwhelming share of royalties, auction premiums, and DMF contributions.
- Mining-related revenue is financially significant for a small number of states (Odisha, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh) but relatively minor in aggregate national fiscal terms (3.4% of all states' revenue receipts).
- India imported minerals worth over ₹10 lakh crore in FY 2025–26, lending some weight to the Centre's argument about domestic production needs.

**On the constitutional question:**

The evidence suggests a genuine legal vulnerability in the 2026 law. The Supreme Court was precise: Parliament can restrict Entry 50 (mineral rights taxation) but not Entry 49 (land taxation). The 2026 Amendment attempts to do both — and extends Central control to mineral-bearing land, which is Entry 18 (land). Whether this survives judicial scrutiny is an open question.

**On the fiscal impact:**

The ₹8,000–10,000 crore estimate from Jharkhand and the ₹15,000 crore estimate from the JMM are state government and party claims, not independent audits. The MBL cess alone was expected to generate ₹7,110 crore annually (per Soren's letter to the PM). The actual loss depends on what conditions the Centre ultimately prescribes. If the Centre allows a modest levy, the loss could be smaller. If it imposes a near-zero cap, the loss could match Soren's estimate. The absence of specific rules at this stage makes precise quantification impossible.

## WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAIN

- **What conditions will the Centre prescribe?** No rules have been notified. The range from "modest ceiling" to "near-zero allowance" is wide.
- **Will states challenge the law in court?** Kerala and Jharkhand have signalled intent to explore legal options, but no formal court filing has been made as of August 18, 2026. The constitutional questions are substantial enough to reach the Supreme Court if filed.
- **What happens to the ₹2 lakh crore in outstanding dues?** The Bill invalidates unpaid dues. Whether courts will permit this retroactive extinguishment — particularly in light of the Supreme Court's 2024 directions allowing 12-year staggered payment — is unresolved.
- **Could lower mining taxation increase production enough to offset lost state revenue?** This is the Centre's implicit theory. The available evidence does not establish this. Mining investment depends on many factors (clearances, infrastructure, demand), and tax is only one.
- **How will this affect District Mineral Foundation (DMF) contributions?** DMF contributions are a percentage of royalty, which is unaffected. But if state-level cesses disappear, the total fiscal package for mining-affected communities may shrink even if DMF stays.
- **Will the law survive a constitutional challenge?** The Centre's extension of control to mineral-bearing land (Entry 18) and the retrospective invalidation provision are the two most likely constitutional flashpoints.
- **When will Presidential assent be granted?** The Bill awaits the President's signature. No date has been announced.

The following distinguishes what has already happened from what is expected or merely possible.

**Already happened:**
- Parliament passed the Bill (Lok Sabha Aug 12, Rajya Sabha Aug 13).
- CM Soren wrote to PM Modi (Aug 13) and to the President (Aug 14).
- CM Satheesan wrote to PM Modi (Aug 17).
- JMM announced a statewide agitation and scheduled a meeting in Ranchi for August 20.
- Jharkhand Finance Minister warned of major financial loss (Aug 14).
- BJP Leader of Opposition in Jharkhand countered that better illegal mining control could increase royalty revenue (Aug 14).

**Announced but not yet executed:**
- Kerala has indicated it is considering political and legal options. No formal petition has been filed.
- Jharkhand's statewide agitation has been announced but not yet conducted.

**Expected:**
- Presidential assent — a formality given the government's majority.
- Centre will frame rules under new Section 13(2)(ta) prescribing conditions for state mineral taxation. No timeline announced. Every week between assent and notification is a week in which states can still collect existing levies.

**Legally possible but uncertain:**
- Constitutional challenge in the Supreme Court by one or more states. The Entry 18/49 vs Entry 50 distinction, the retrospective provision, and the excessive delegation question are the strongest grounds.
- States may attempt to accelerate collection of existing dues before rules are notified.

**Uncertain:**
- Whether increased mining investment (if any) will offset lost state revenue.
- Whether the Centre will set a generous or restrictive ceiling on state levies.
- Whether other mineral-producing states beyond Jharkhand, Kerala, Odisha, Tamil Nadu, and Karnataka will join opposition.

## BOTTOM LINE

The MMDR Amendment Act, 2026 does not abolish state mineral taxation. It subordinates it to Central government discretion. For Jharkhand, which had built a fiscal architecture around the ₹14,656 crore MBL Cess, this is an existential fiscal question — not a policy tweak. For the Centre, it is about creating a single national framework for an industry it says is burdened by fragmented levies. For constitutional lawyers, it is a test of whether Parliament can use Entry 54 to reach Entry 49 — a question the Supreme Court specifically said it could not. The real answer will come not from Parliament but from the courts.

- Outstanding pre-Amendment state mineral levies are invalidated (unpaid dues); collected amounts are not refunded.

## COUNTERARGUMENTS

**Centre's case:** Uniform taxation reduces regulatory fragmentation, lowers mining costs, attracts investment, and ensures mineral security. States already receive ~90% of mining revenue. Existing levies continue — the Centre only wants to set a ceiling. India's ₹10 lakh crore mineral import bill underscores the need for a competitive domestic framework.

**States' case:** The law extinguishes a constitutionally validated fiscal tool. States bear mining's environmental and social costs and need revenue to compensate affected communities. The retrospective invalidation of unpaid dues benefits non-compliant miners at the expense of compliant ones. The delegation of rule-making to the Centre without guiding principles is constitutionally suspect.

**Industry's case (supporting the Bill):** Multiple and excessive state levies — particularly the MBL Cess — created fiscal unpredictability and raised the cost of mineral extraction. The amendments provide certainty.

**Industry's case (against retrospective provision):** The ₹2 lakh crore in outstanding dues represented a known liability. Companies that settled early are now at a disadvantage compared to those that delayed.
